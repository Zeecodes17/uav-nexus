Pilot Emergency Actions

Human-centered emergency actions when systems fail, information is incomplete, or stress compromises decision-making. This page applies to all failure types.

1. Primary Rule

Do not rush inputs. Rushed inputs cause most secondary failures.

When something goes wrong, the aircraft is often still controllable for a short time. Panic removes that margin faster than the failure itself.

2. Stabilize First

Stability before navigation.
  • Reduce aggressive stick inputs
  • Stabilize attitude and altitude
  • Accept small drift instead of fighting it

A stable aircraft gives you time. Time gives you options.

3. Rapid Assessment (5–10 seconds)

Ask yourself:
  • Do I still have control authority?
  • Is the aircraft responding predictably?
  • Is battery margin decreasing rapidly?

You do not need perfect answers — only enough to choose the safer direction.

4. Simplify the Task

Remove objectives, reduce workload.
  • Abandon mission goals
  • Avoid complex navigation
  • Choose the shortest safe path

Complexity increases pilot error under stress.

5. Commit to a Conservative Outcome

A controlled landing is a successful outcome.
  • Select the safest reachable area
  • Do not chase a “perfect” landing spot
  • Accept minor damage over loss of control

Most serious losses occur while attempting to save the mission, not the aircraft.

6. Common Pilot Errors Under Stress

Error Why It’s Dangerous
Rapid mode switching Creates conflicting control logic
Over-correcting inputs Destabilizes already degraded systems
Fixating on telemetry Reduces visual awareness
Delaying landing Consumes remaining margin

7. After the Event

Secure, then analyze.
  • Disarm and disconnect power
  • Note conditions and symptoms
  • Do not immediately re-fly

Many emergencies are prevented during planning. Review battery margin and endurance tools before flight.

Good pilots do not avoid failures. They recognize them early, simplify decisions, and choose survivable outcomes.
